## Abstract

[Unstructured, 229 words as drafted. The BJSW abstract specification is not in the guidelines text I have. Confirm the word limit and whether structured headings are required before submission.]

Children's social care records are, for many care-experienced adults, the only sustained account of their own childhood. Care-experienced adults report those records as fragmentary, dominated by deficit, and heavily redacted at the point of release. One response gaining ground in children's services is child-directed recording: writing the case record to the child, in the second person, in ordinary words. This paper argues that the reform has been described more broadly than it acts. Writing to the child changes the register of the record and the visibility of its author. The harms reported by care-experienced adults are mostly harms of content, of retention and of access, and those are not reached by a change of pronoun. The paper makes three arguments. First, plain language and second-person address are separate proposals that the field has bundled, and the case for the first is considerably stronger than the case for the second. Second, the identity function of the record has been treated as achievable through style, when it depends on what is recorded, what survives, and what is releasable. Third, the evidence base for child-directed recording is practice conviction rather than evaluation, and no located study measures what the reform does to record content. A comparative argument follows: register reform and access reform are separate projects, and the driver that produced a jurisdiction's record reform appears to determine which project it undertook.

## Introduction

A social work record is written by one person about another, inside a relationship where the writer holds statutory power over the person written about, and it is read, if it is read at all, by someone the writer will never meet, often twenty or thirty years later. That reader is frequently the child the record is about. For many care-experienced adults the file is the only sustained account of their own childhood that exists.

What that reader finds has been described consistently enough, by care-experienced adults themselves and by the researchers working with them, to be treated as established. Files are fragmentary. They record deficit far more fully than they record anything else. They arrive redacted, sometimes to the point where the person's own family has been removed from the account of their own life. The process of obtaining them is itself reported as distressing [VERIFY: MIRRA project outputs (Memory, Identity, Rights in Records, Access), University College London, in partnership with the Care Leavers' Association; associated with Shepherd, Hoyle, Flinn and Lomas. Exact output titles and years required before citation].

One response, which has moved from local initiative to something closer to policy in parts of England and which has a longer history in Denmark, is to change how the record is written. Child-directed recording, or writing to the child, replaces third-person case notes with entries addressed to the child in the second person, in ordinary words, signed by a named worker. The case made for it is that it renders the record legible to the person who will eventually read it, that it forces the writer to own what they have written, and that it changes what a writer is willing to commit to the page. The last of those is the strongest version of the claim: that a practitioner who would type "unknown males" into a form field will not write it to a child.

I argue that this reform has been described more broadly than it acts, and that the description has been allowed to stand because nobody has measured it.

The argument runs in four stages. First, the record is asked to serve two purposes that produce different documents, and the reported harms come from the dominance of one purpose rather than from the grammar in which it is written. Second, the field has bundled two separate proposals, plain language and second-person address, and the case for plain language is strong in a way that the case for the second person is not; separating them requires a three-way distinction between authorship, address and attribution that the literature does not currently make. Third, the mechanism claimed for direct address, that it restrains what a writer will say, is double-edged, and the possibility that it suppresses severity while reading as warmer is the sharpest empirical claim available in this field and remains untested. Fourth, access and redaction constitute a separate reform project, and a jurisdiction that has done one has not thereby done the other.

The paper is a critical conceptual review. It does not report new data, and it is explicit throughout about the difference between what the literature establishes, what it asserts, and what nobody has yet looked at.

## Scope and terms

This paper is written primarily about England. That matters, and the distinction is not decorative. Scotland has conducted its own care review and legislates separately [VERIFY: Independent Care Review (Scotland, 2020) *The Promise*; confirm what it says about lifelong access to records before attaching any specific claim]. Australian record reform emerged from a national inquiry into institutional abuse and sits under different legislation and different archival arrangements. Where I draw on Danish, Australian or Scottish material I say so, and I say what does not travel.

By "the record" I mean the case file held by the local authority about a child receiving statutory services, now almost always an electronic record within a case management system. I distinguish it throughout from the products of life story work, which are separate artefacts made with the child for the child, and from the words and pictures explanations used to tell a child what has happened in language they can follow [VERIFY: words and pictures associated with Essex and Turnell within the Signs of Safety approach; confirm authorship, the primary text and date].

"Child-directed recording" and "writing to the child" are used interchangeably here for the practice of composing case record entries addressed to the child in the second person. I use "care-experienced adult" for people who have received their records as adults, and "the child" for the subject of a record at the time it is written, because the two are the same person separated by the period that makes this problem what it is.

The review has limits that follow from how it was assembled. It is not systematic. There is no search protocol, no inclusion criteria applied across databases, and no assessment of study quality against a published tool. It draws on English-language material only, which in a comparative argument about jurisdictions is a real constraint rather than a formal one, since the Danish, Norwegian and Dutch positions are likely to be documented substantially in languages this review did not cover. Much of what governs recording practice sits in unpublished local guidance, procedure manuals and case management system configuration, none of which is retrievable through the published literature. Where the argument depends on grey literature or on practice accounts rather than peer-reviewed work, I say so at the point of use.

## Two purposes, one document

The oldest fault line in this field is what the record is for, and the rest of the debate sits on top of it.

The record is an accountability document. It evidences decisions, it must be defensible under inspection and complaint, and it may be read in court. It is also, for the person it is about, the only sustained account of their childhood. Those two purposes generate different documents.

Accountability rewards completeness on the organisation's terms, hedged attribution, the passive voice, and the recording of process alongside or instead of substance. It rewards writing that cannot be used against the writer. Broadhurst and colleagues examined error and blame in initial assessment in children's services and describe the conditions in which defensive practice becomes rational for the practitioner [VERIFY: Broadhurst et al., approximately 2010, *British Journal of Social Work*; exact authorship, title, year, volume and pages required]. Munro (2011) made the wider version of this argument in her review of child protection, finding that recording and compliance systems had displaced direct work with children, so that the time and attention available for the child went into the account of the work rather than the work.

Identity rewards the opposite. It rewards narrative, specificity, a named author, continuity across placements and workers, and the retention of ordinary detail that no accountability process has ever asked for: what the child was like, what they liked, who mattered to them, what was said in the car on the way back. The finding that files are fragmentary and dominated by deficit is what the accountability purpose produces when it is the only purpose in play. That is the document doing what it was built to do, rather than a failure of individual writing.

Two things follow that the field has not resolved.

The first is a genuine contradiction and I do not think the literature settles it. The two readerships are separated by decades, and the second reader is not present when the document is written. A practitioner recording a strategy discussion is writing for a manager, a chair, possibly a court, and possibly a thirty-year-old who does not yet exist in any form the writer can picture. Asking one document to serve both is not obviously possible, and the field has mostly proceeded as though it is.

The second is that the profession has already answered this question once, in the other direction. Life story work exists because the file could not do the identity job [VERIFY: Rose, Richard, on therapeutic life story work; identify and confirm the primary text and date before citing]. Words and pictures exists because the file could not explain events to a child in words the child could use. Both are separate artefacts, made deliberately, with the child, for the child. If the correct answer to the identity problem is a second document, then child-directed recording is solving a problem that has already been solved differently, and the interesting question becomes why the field is now trying to make the file do a job it previously built other tools to do.

I do not think that question can be answered from the published literature, and I raise it as a limit on what follows rather than a point I can close.

## Authorship, address and attribution

The case for child-directed recording is usually put as a single proposition: write to the child, in plain words, and sign it. That bundles three changes which can be made independently, and separating them is where most of the analytic work in this paper is done.

**Authorship** is the grammatical position of the writer. A record can be written in the first person, where the worker says "I visited", "I asked", "I was not able to see the bedroom", or in an impersonal register where "a home visit was undertaken". First-person authorship makes the writer present and accountable in the text.

**Address** is how the subject of the record is referred to. The record can name the child in the third person, or address them in the second person as "you".

**Attribution** is whose judgement is recorded and on what basis. An attributed record says who concluded what, from what evidence, and distinguishes an observation from an inference from a report by a third party. "The house was chaotic" is unattributed. "I saw unwashed dishes across the kitchen floor and three days of post unopened; I concluded the household was not managing basic routines" is attributed, and a reader can disagree with the conclusion while accepting the observation.

These three are routinely presented as one reform. They are not. A record can be written in the first person, attributed carefully, in plain words, and still refer to the child in the third person. That document delivers legibility, ownership and the ability to contest a conclusion, without addressing the child at all. Conversely, a record can be addressed to the child throughout and remain unattributed: "you were living in a chaotic home" carries exactly the judgement-presented-as-observation that the original phrasing carried, with a warmer surface.

Once the three are separated, the defences offered for writing to the child sort themselves. Legibility is delivered mainly by plain language, and plain language is a proposal about vocabulary and syntax that does not require the second person. Ownership is delivered by first-person authorship and by a named signature, neither of which requires the second person. The ability to contest what was said about you is delivered by attribution, which requires the second person least of all.

That leaves the second person carrying one distinctive claim of its own: that addressing the child changes what the writer is willing to write. Everything else attributed to child-directed recording is available without it.

This matters for how the field reads its own evidence. Where practitioners report that writing to the child improved their records, and where care-experienced readers report that a record was easier to read, the improvement is consistent with plain language and attribution doing the work while the pronoun rides along. No study I could locate separates them [SOURCE NEEDED: any study that distinguishes the effects of plain language, first-person authorship and second-person address, rather than evaluating the bundle]. Until one does, the field cannot attribute its reported gains to the component it has made most visible.

The practice consequence is immediate. If plain language and attribution deliver most of the benefit and carry none of the risks discussed below, then the safe reform is the one nobody is branding.

## What direct address does to content

The distinctive claim for the second person is a claim about restraint. The argument is that phrases like "unknown males" are harder to put in front of a child than into a form field, and that a writer who pictures the reader will write differently.

I accept the mechanism. My argument is that it does not run only in the direction its proponents assume.

If addressing the reader restrains what the writer commits to the page, the restraint operates on severity as well as on dehumanising shorthand. The writer imagining a child reading is imagining a child of a particular age, usually the age the child is now, and pitching to that reader. Detail about sexual harm, about what a parent was observed to be doing, about the specificity of an injury, is exactly the material a writer would soften for a young reader. The resulting entry can be warmer, more readable, and less informative about risk, and it can be all three at once without the writer noticing, because the felt improvement in the writing is real.

This produces a testable prediction, and it is the sharpest empirical claim available in this field: records written to the child will contain less specific risk information than records written in the third person, holding the case population constant. I could find no study testing it [SOURCE NEEDED: any evaluation of child-directed recording measuring effects on record content, rather than practitioner satisfaction or reader response]. The design is not difficult. A single local authority that has changed its recording policy provides a before-and-after comparison within one population, one system and one workforce, and record content can be coded for specificity of risk description, attribution of source, retention of third-party detail, and hedging. The absence of that study, in a field that has adopted the practice, is the most substantial gap this review identifies.

Three further objections belong here, and I hold them open rather than resolving them.

The first is consent. Direct address creates a relationship on the page. The writer addresses the child as "you", in a register of intimacy, inside a relationship where the writer holds statutory power over where the child lives and who they see. The child did not ask to be written to, cannot read the document at the time it is written, and cannot decline the address. A record that says "I know this was hard for you, and I want you to know I was thinking about you" is written by the person who is also, in the same week, recommending that the child does not go home. Whether care-experienced adults experience that as connection or as intrusion is an empirical question that has not been asked in this form [SOURCE NEEDED: evidence on what care-experienced adults say about second-person entries specifically, as distinct from their records in general].

The second is the multiplicity of readers. The file is read by successor social workers, by independent reviewing officers, by panels, by courts and by the child. A document optimised for one of those readers may serve the others badly, and the successor worker inheriting a case at short notice needs the risk history retrievable at speed. Again, no data.

The third is the possibility of a specific harm that the reform's own logic creates. If the record is written as a letter from someone who cared, and the person later obtains it alongside the unredacted account of what was decided and what was not done, the warmth of the register becomes evidence of a gap between what was said to them and what was done for them. A cold record is a poor account of a childhood. A warm record that was not matched by action may be a worse one. I have no evidence for this and offer it as a hypothesis that the same evaluation design could test.

There is a further constraint that sits underneath all of it. White, Hall and Peckover argued, in work on the Integrated Children's System and the Common Assessment Framework, that electronic recording systems shape what practitioners are able to think, not only what they are able to write [VERIFY: White, Hall and Peckover, approximately 2009, possibly *British Journal of Social Work*; exact title, year, volume and pages required. This is the strongest theoretical link available to the mode-of-address argument and is worth the time to find]. If that is right, mode of address is a change applied to the surface of a document whose categories have already been fixed by the system that holds it. A dropdown does not become more truthful when the free-text box beneath it is addressed to the child. Reform of the pronoun without reform of the form is a change to the part of the record the practitioner controls least.

What follows for practice is narrower than the field's enthusiasm suggests. A practitioner can adopt first-person authorship and full attribution tomorrow, in any system, with no policy change, and gain most of what child-directed recording promises. A practitioner adopting second-person address should be asked, explicitly and in supervision, whether the entry they have just written contains the same risk detail it would have contained in the third person.

## Description, omission and anti-oppressive practice

Description carries the same problem in a form that address does not reach, and it is where the record does most of its distributional work.

"Chaotic", "unkempt", "aggressive" and "unknown males" present the writer's judgement as observation. What a writer treats as usual, safe or good enough is set by their own position rather than by anything in the room, which is where the Social GGRRAAACCEEESSS do work as a practice tool rather than as a training exercise. The writer's race, class, culture, faith and family experience set the threshold at which a household becomes "chaotic" in a sentence that reads as a description of the household rather than as a judgement about it. Second-person address changes none of this. "Your home was chaotic" preserves the judgement and removes the distance that might have prompted a later reader to question it.

Class sets that threshold most directly, because home conditions are recorded against a standard of household order that is nowhere written down and is not held consistently across a workforce.

Gender is visible in this paper's own example. "Unknown males" names a category by sex and by the absence of information, and it names no person. The same file will ordinarily name the mother, record her decisions, and hold her answerable for what the unknown males did. The grammar carries the allocation: the men are a hazard the household contains, the woman is an agent who failed to exclude them, and neither proposition has been attributed to anyone or tested against anything. A record can be addressed to the child in warm second-person prose and reproduce that structure intact [SOURCE NEEDED: empirical work on the gendered allocation of responsibility in child protection records, including "failure to protect" recorded against mothers and the recording of fathers as unnamed or absent. I am confident this literature exists in England and cannot name it].

Race and ethnicity attract the strongest version of the concern, that evaluative descriptors appear disproportionately in records about Black and mixed heritage families. I am not able to source it here [SOURCE NEEDED: empirical study of the distribution of evaluative descriptors in children's social care records by ethnicity in England. This claim must not appear in a submitted draft without it].

Disability enters twice, and the second way is easy to miss. Parental disability and parental learning disability are recorded predominantly in the deficit register the document already favours, so a parent's support needs and their alleged failings arrive in the file in the same vocabulary. Separately, a record written for eventual reading assumes a reader who can read it. Plain language, and the availability of the file in accessible formats, serve a disabled reader. Second-person address does not, and a disabled care-experienced adult requesting their file encounters the same disclosure process as anyone else, with the same absence of support at the point of release.

Age is what the severity argument in the previous section is about. A writer pitching to the child's present age is making an anti-oppressive judgement about comprehension and a risk judgement about disclosure in the same sentence, without separating them.

Religion, culture, language and sexuality enter through omission rather than through description, and omission is the harder problem. Article 8 protects the child's right to preserve their identity. A file that never records what language was spoken at home, what the family observed and when, what the child was called by people who loved them, or how a young person described their own sexuality, leaves the adult reader without the material to reconstruct any of it. No accountability process asks for those things, which is precisely why the accountability document does not contain them.

That is the clearest case in this paper of something a change of register cannot touch. A practitioner cannot write warmly about a fact they did not record, and the second person offers no prompt to record it. What would prompt it is a recording framework that treats identity material as required content rather than as colour, which is a change to what the form asks for rather than to how the answer is phrased.

## Whose need does writing to the child meet

Practitioners who write to children report that it changes their relationship to the task. The record stops being an administrative obligation and becomes something closer to a communication. That is a real and reported gain, and it is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, particularly in the light of Munro's (2011) finding that recording had displaced direct work. A reform that makes the record feel like part of the relationship rather than a subtraction from it is addressing something practitioners genuinely experience.

The question this paper puts is whose need that gain meets.

The satisfaction a practitioner takes from writing warmly to a child is evidence about the practitioner. It is not, by itself, evidence about the record, and it is not evidence about the child. This is the standing problem in helping work: the feeling of having helped is generated inside the helper and is available whether or not the help landed. A worker who finishes an entry addressed to a child, and feels that they have done something for that child, has had an experience that the child will not have for another twenty years, and may not have then.

The evidence base repeats the same structure. No evaluation of child-directed recording could be located that measures effects on record content rather than practitioner satisfaction. The field has measured the thing that is easy to measure and that returns a favourable answer, which happens to be the practitioner's experience of the reform. The reader it is for has not been asked about it specifically, and the document it changes has not been examined.

There is a cost on the practitioner's side as well, and the reform as currently promoted does not acknowledge it. A worker writing to a child in the second person is writing intimately in a document that may be read in court, quoted back in a complaint, or examined in an inspection. The accountability pressure that produces defensive recording has not been removed. Asking practitioners to write with more of themselves visible, into a document whose risks to them are unchanged, moves the cost of the reform onto the individual worker and leaves the organisational conditions that produced defensive recording in place. If the record is to become a more personal document, the organisation has to change what it does to workers whose personal writing is later found wanting, and I am not aware that any implementation has addressed this [SOURCE NEEDED: any account of how organisations implementing child-directed recording have handled the accountability exposure created for individual practitioners].

## Access, redaction and the second project

The literature on access is thicker than the literature on writing, and most of it sits in archival science rather than social work.

The mechanism is legal. Subject access provisions under the Data Protection Act 2018 and the UK General Data Protection Regulation give a person a right of access to personal data held about them, and the same framework protects the personal data of third parties who appear in the same document. In a children's social care file, the third parties are the person's family. The result is not contested and does not need to be argued: a person receives the account of their own childhood with the other people in it removed or obscured.

What the archival research adds is that the process itself does harm, rather than merely delivering a poor product. The MIRRA project's contribution, working participatively with care-experienced adults alongside the Care Leavers' Association, is the finding that the experience of requesting, waiting for and receiving a redacted file is itself distressing, which reframes access as a practice question rather than an administrative one [VERIFY: MIRRA outputs and their exact findings; confirm titles, years and which findings belong to which output]. Golding, writing as both a care leaver and an academic, treats records as sites of power and works on the distance between the official file and the person's own account of their life [VERIFY: Frank Golding, Australia; confirm institutional affiliation, key publications and dates, and the relationship to the Setting the Record Straight for the Rights of the Child project, possibly with Cate O'Neill].

The Australian position was shaped by inquiry. The Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse, which ran from 2013 to 2017, made recommendations on the creation, retention and accessibility of records [VERIFY: exact report, year and recommendation content. Do not cite recommendation numbers without checking them]. Services such as Find and Connect exist to help care leavers locate records that may be held across multiple organisations, some of them defunct [VERIFY: Find and Connect, Australia; confirm the operating body and scope].

Retention determines whether any of this is available at all, and it precedes every other question in this section. A record that has been destroyed cannot be redacted, contested or read. I am not able to state the retention period applying to care records in England with the confidence a published paper requires [SOURCE NEEDED: statutory or guidance retention period for children's social care records in England, with the instrument that sets it].

The argument this section supports is structural. Everything described here is untouched by mode of address. A record written to a child in 2026 in warm, plain, second-person prose, requested by that person in 2050, is disclosed under the same subject access framework, redacted by the same third-party rules, by an organisation applying the same retention schedule. It arrives with the family removed. The register in which it was written does not alter a single one of those steps.

Access reform and register reform are therefore separate projects with separate mechanisms. Access reform operates on law, retention policy, disclosure practice and the support offered at the point of release. Register reform operates on what a practitioner types. A jurisdiction that has done one has not thereby done the other, and the two are not substitutes.

Participation belongs here rather than with writing, for the same reason. Article 12 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child gives the child the right to be heard in matters affecting them, and Article 8 protects the child's right to preserve their identity [VERIFY: United Nations (1989) *Convention on the Rights of the Child*; confirm the exact wording of Articles 8 and 12 before quoting or paraphrasing closely]. Whether being heard extends to what is recorded about you, and to a route for challenging it, is largely unaddressed in practice. Where mechanisms exist they are complaint and correction procedures built for factual error: a wrong date, a wrong name, a wrong address. The harm care-experienced adults report sits in characterisation rather than in fact. Being described as aggressive is not a factual error that a correction procedure can process, and it is the sentence that the person is still carrying thirty years later.

A record written to the child in the second person does not give the child any more purchase on this than a record written in the third person. The child cannot read either at the time. What would give them purchase is a route to contest characterisation, and a mechanism for a person's own account to be attached to the file rather than replacing what is there. That is an access and governance reform, not a writing reform, and it is the one that follows most directly from what care-experienced adults say.

## Why jurisdictions differ

The comparative pattern is the most publishable part of this argument and the part most likely to fall apart under proper examination. I state it as a hypothesis.

Different jurisdictions arrived at record reform through different drivers, and the driver appears to determine what gets reformed.

Australia came to records through national inquiry into institutional abuse. The question the inquiry was answering was evidential: what happened, who knew, and what survives to prove it. The reform that followed concentrated on creation, retention and evidential integrity, on making sure a record exists, is complete, and is still there decades later.

England came substantially through care-leaver advocacy and archival research, with the MIRRA project as the clearest instance, and the question being answered was about the experience of reading your own file. The reform work went into access, into redaction practice, and into the support offered when a file is handed over.

Denmark, on the only account available to this review, came through direct work with care-experienced young people, and produced principles for how case records should be written [VERIFY: Mette Larsen, Denmark; the nine principles for writing in case records. No publication details available. This is currently sourced only to an unpublished seminar deck and cannot be cited in a submitted paper in that form]. The reform is about register and authorship.

If the pattern holds, the conclusion follows directly and is worth stating: register reform and access reform are separate projects, most jurisdictions have completed at most one, and a country that has fixed access has not thereby fixed what the file says. England's reforms have concentrated on the point of release, decades after the writing. Denmark's have concentrated on the writing, with no account available here of what happens at release.

Three things stop this being a finding.

The units may not be comparable. A national statutory duty, a set of national guidance, and a locally adopted practice principle are different kinds of object, and comparing them as though they were equivalent policy positions would be an error. I cannot currently say which Danish practice is [SOURCE NEEDED: whether Danish child-directed recording is statutory, national guidance, or local practice, and which body issued it].

The sample is small and selected. Three jurisdictions, chosen because material about them was available in English, cannot establish a relationship between reform driver and reform content. Four further jurisdictions that would test the pattern are absent from this review entirely [SOURCE NEEDED: Norwegian, Dutch, New Zealand and Irish positions on record creation, register and access]. Ireland in particular had its own institutional abuse inquiries and would be a direct test of whether the inquiry-driven pattern reproduces.

The pattern is tidy, and tidy patterns in comparative policy are usually the ones that fall apart on close reading. The most likely failure is that each jurisdiction has done more than the English-language material shows, and that the apparent specialisation is an artefact of what gets published and translated.

What would test it is a documented comparison in which the reform documents themselves are the data: for each jurisdiction, the driver, the instrument, and what the instrument actually requires, coded for whether it addresses creation, retention, register, attribution, access or contestation. That is a feasible piece of work and it would either establish the pattern or dispose of it.

## What follows for practice

**For practitioners.** Separate the three positions in your own writing. Write in the first person, so it is clear who did what. Attribute every conclusion to whoever reached it and say what it rests on, distinguishing what you saw, what you were told, and what you concluded. Use ordinary words. Then treat address as a fourth, separate decision rather than something that follows automatically from the first three.

- Record the specificity of risk to the same standard regardless of who you imagine reading. If an entry addressed to a child contains less detail than the third-person version would have, that is a reason to change the entry, not the standard.
- Replace judgement presented as observation. Name what you saw, heard or smelled, name who told you, then state your conclusion as a conclusion with your name on it.
- Ask in supervision, on any entry written to a child, what has been left out because of who it was addressed to.

**For local authorities and case management system owners.** The pronoun is the part of the record the practitioner controls most and the system controls least. Audit the form before reforming the address: if the fields, dropdowns and mandatory categories determine what can be recorded, changing the free-text register alters the smallest available part of the document.

- Build a route for a child or a care-experienced adult to contest characterisation, not only factual error, and a mechanism for their own account to be attached to the file alongside what is already there rather than replacing it.
- Treat subject access as social work practice rather than an administrative function, including a named worker available to read the file alongside the person receiving it.
- If you are implementing child-directed recording, say explicitly what protection a practitioner has when a personally written entry is later examined in court, in a complaint or in an inspection. Without that, the reform transfers its risk to the individual worker.

**For people who use social work services, and for their families.** The argument here has a direct implication and it is not a comfortable one. A file that reads more kindly is not thereby a fuller file, and a person asking for their records has an interest in what was written down and kept, which is separable from how it was phrased. The questions that determine what a person eventually receives are how long the record is retained, whether identity material was recorded at all, what is removed at disclosure, and whether anyone sits with them while they read it.

- Family members have a distinct interest that the current framework treats only as an obstacle. Under subject access, a parent, sibling or grandparent is third-party personal data to be redacted from the child's file, and the same relative may hold the only account of the family that the record does not contain. ## Ethics and the position this paper is written from

This is a conceptual paper. It reports no empirical data, involved no participants, and required no ethical approval. It uses no case material, and the descriptors examined throughout are phrases in general circulation in English children's services rather than extracts from any identifiable record.

The ethical difficulty is elsewhere, and it applies to the paper itself.

This is an argument about what care-experienced people need from records that are about them, and it is not written with care-experienced people. It draws on participative research in which care-experienced adults set the questions, and then makes a further set of claims that those adults have not been asked about. The central empirical proposition here, that second-person entries may read as warmer while carrying less, is a proposition about how a care-experienced reader would receive a document, advanced by someone who will not receive one. I can state the limit, and stating it does not remove it.

What would resolve it is authorship rather than acknowledgement: the questions this paper raises about second-person entries are answerable by care-experienced adults and by nobody else, and the work that answers them should be led or co-authored by people who have read their own files [CONFIRM: whether you want to approach care-experienced co-authors before submission, and whether to say so in the paper. BJSW states that it prioritises submissions incorporating the perspectives of people with personal experience and can offer additional support to such submissions].

I write as a social work practitioner who has authored records of the kind this paper is about, and who has written entries that a person may one day read and find thin [CONFIRM: how you want your position described, and whether to name your role. Anonymise for double-blind review]. That position gives me access to why practitioners find writing to the child worth doing, and it is also the position with the most to gain from believing that a change of register is sufficient.

## Limits of the argument

This is a conceptual review without a systematic search, and its coverage reflects what was available in English and what is published rather than what exists. Practice guidance governing recording is largely unpublished and local, and it is likely that implementations of child-directed recording contain safeguards this review has not seen.

The paper makes no claim about the effects of child-directed recording on record content, because no evidence about those effects could be located. The severity prediction is a hypothesis derived from the reform's own account of its mechanism, and it may be wrong in either direction: address may restrain dehumanising shorthand without touching risk detail, or it may do neither.

The comparative argument is a hypothesis about three jurisdictions and should be read as a proposal for research rather than a finding.

The paper's standing is limited in the way set out above. It argues about the interests of care-experienced people without being written with them, and its central proposition about how second-person entries would be received is the proposition it is least qualified to advance.

Nothing here argues against writing to children in ways they can understand. The claim is narrower: that the field has bundled a strong proposal with a weak one, has treated a change of register as a solution to problems of content, retention and access, and has adopted a practice without measuring what it does to the document it changes.

## Conclusion

Care-experienced adults describe records that are fragmentary, dominated by deficit, and redacted at the point they finally arrive. Child-directed recording responds to the way those records read. The response is worth having, and most of what it delivers, legibility, ownership and the ability to contest a conclusion, comes from plain language, first-person authorship and careful attribution, none of which require the second person.

The second person carries one claim of its own, that it changes what a writer will commit to the page, and that claim runs in both directions. It may remove the shorthand that dehumanises. It may also remove the detail that makes a record useful to the person who eventually reads it and to the worker who inherits the case. Nobody has looked.

Meanwhile the file is retained under a schedule the writer does not set, and released under disclosure rules that remove the person's family from the account of their own childhood. A record addressed to a child arrives, thirty years later, redacted by the same rules as any other. Register reform and access reform are different projects, and the more comfortable of the two is the one the profession has chosen to do.
