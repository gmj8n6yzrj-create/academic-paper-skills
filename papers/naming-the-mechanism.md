# Naming the mechanism: what behavioural science offers accounts of how social workers effect change

A narrative review

**Draft for discussion. See the source audit note at the end before submission.**

---

## Abstract

Statutory social work in England is organised around an expectation that families will change, and change within timescales set by the court and the child protection plan. The profession's account of how that change happens rests largely on the relationship between worker and family. Relationship-based practice describes the medium in which change occurs, and the observational research shows that what workers actually do inside that relationship varies widely. Neither body of work specifies the mechanism, which leaves practice difficult to teach, difficult to supervise and difficult to test. This narrative review reads four bodies of behavioural science against that gap: capability, opportunity and motivation as the conditions a behaviour requires; habit as repetition of a specified action in a stable context; shame as the condition that determines whether help can be received at all; and scarcity as a constraint on the cognitive capacity that services routinely demand. The review argues that behavioural science names mechanisms social workers already use without describing, and that naming them makes practice teachable and auditable. It also argues that the individual frame of much behavioural science fails on contact with poverty and statutory power, and that the shame literature is where social work has most to give the behavioural sciences rather than take from them. Implications for planning, supervision and research are set out.

**Keywords:** child protection, behaviour change, habit formation, shame, motivational interviewing, relationship-based practice, help-seeking

## Introduction

Every child protection plan in England is a behaviour change intervention. It names things that must be different, usually adult behaviours, and it sets a period in which they must become different. Social workers are then held accountable for whether the change happened, in review conferences, in supervision and in court.

The profession's account of how it produces that change is thin. Asked how a family changed, social workers commonly answer that they built a relationship. The answer is true and it is under-specified to the point where it cannot be taught with any precision, cannot be supervised against, and cannot be evaluated. A worker who succeeds and a worker who fails will both report that they built a relationship, and both may be telling the truth.

The claim of this paper is that behavioural science names mechanisms that social workers already use, and that naming them changes what can be planned for, supervised and tested. The mechanisms in question are ordinary: a specified action, repeated in a stable context, until it needs less deliberate effort; a conversation that draws out a person's own reasons for changing rather than supplying the worker's; the removal of friction from an intended behaviour; and the management of shame, which determines whether any of the others can operate.

The paper makes a second claim, which qualifies the first. Behavioural science arrives in social care carrying an individual frame, and that frame fails on contact with the material conditions and the statutory power that characterise child protection work. Chater and Loewenstein (2023) argue that behavioural public policy has concentrated on individual-level solutions at the expense of systemic ones, and that this has been the field's central error. Their argument applies directly here. A parent who does not attend an appointment may lack a bus fare, a phone with credit, or the cognitive bandwidth that Mani and colleagues (2013) show is consumed by financial scarcity. Reading that parent as unmotivated is a diagnostic error before it is an ethical one.

The paper therefore asks what survives when behavioural science is taken into a setting where the helper holds the power to remove a child, where the person being helped is frequently poor, and where needing help is itself a source of shame.

## The review and its limits

This is a narrative review, organised by argument rather than by chronology or study design. It does not use a systematic search protocol, and it does not appraise study quality against a formal instrument. The literature it draws on is purposively selected across four fields that do not routinely cite one another: social work practice research, health behaviour change, the psychology of shame, and the behavioural economics of scarcity.

Three limits follow from the approach and should be read alongside everything below.

The literature is English-language and skewed to England, the United States and Australia. Where evidence comes from another jurisdiction, I say so, because child protection systems differ in ways that change what a finding means. English social work is generalist at the point of qualification, and English child protection carries statutory duties under a threshold framework that has no direct American equivalent.

The behavioural science drawn on here was, with few exceptions, produced in health settings with volunteer participants who chose their own target behaviour. That is close to the opposite of a parent subject to a child protection plan. I treat that difference as the central analytic problem rather than as a footnote.

I write as a practitioner and manager in English children's services, and the questions I brought to this literature came from watching plans fail in ways that looked patterned rather than random. A reader from a different position would probably group this literature differently.

## What the profession says about how change happens

Relationship-based practice is the profession's dominant account. Ruch (2005) argues that practitioners need reflective capacity if relationship-based work is to be more than an aspiration, and locates the approach against bureaucratic responses to complicated family problems. The edited collection by Ruch, Turney and Ward (2018) develops this into a model for practice across settings. The position holds that human relationships are the means by which social work does anything at all.

The difficulty is what the position leaves out. It describes the medium and the conditions, and says comparatively little about the actions inside the relationship that produce a change in what a parent does on a Tuesday morning. This is a gap in specification rather than a disagreement about values.

The observational research shows what fills that gap in practice. Forrester, Kershaw, Moss and Hughes (2008) analysed how social workers talked to parents and found a confrontational style used so consistently that the authors read it as systemic rather than individual. The companion study by Forrester, McCambridge, Waissbein and Rollnick (2008) reached similar conclusions about how concerns were raised. Both studies worked from simulated encounters rather than observation of real families `[VERIFY: confirm the method description against both papers before submission]`, which limits what they show about practice in the home, and both are now more than fifteen years old.

Forrester and colleagues (2019) addressed part of that limitation by observing practice with 127 families and relating worker skill to family outcomes. They report an association between the communication skills drawn from motivational interviewing and parent-reported engagement and wellbeing. The design was observational and the outcomes were largely parent-reported at a single point, so the direction of the association cannot be settled from it. A worker may be more skilled with a parent who is easier to engage. The study nonetheless does something the relationship literature does not: it makes the worker's behaviour a measurable variable and connects it to something that happens to the family.

What happens to that variable once workers are qualified is less encouraging. Wilkins, Forrester and Grant (2017) recorded 34 supervision case discussions and found them functioning mainly as management oversight, with limited room for reflection, emotional support or critical thinking. The sample is small and drawn from a limited number of authorities, and supervision practice varies, but the finding matters for this argument. If the worker's own behaviour is the active ingredient, and supervision does not examine the worker's behaviour, then the active ingredient is unsupervised.

Practice frameworks have been the sector's main answer, and the evidence for them is weaker than their adoption suggests. Baginsky and colleagues (2020), working across the evaluations of Signs of Safety, report significant gaps in the evidence that the model improves practice or outcomes for children, and observations in one implementing organisation recorded home visits in which no identified element of the model appeared in the interaction at all `[SOURCE NEEDED: this claim, and the figure for how many English authorities use the model, come from evaluation reporting I could not open directly. Attach the primary evaluation before this paragraph goes out.]` A profession can adopt an approach at scale without the approach reaching the point of contact with a family.

Ferguson (2016, 2017) gets closer to that point of contact than most research does, following practitioners on visits and attending to movement, atmosphere and the body as well as talk. His account of how children become invisible in day-to-day practice shows that what happens in a home is shaped by things no plan records: whether the worker got past the doorway, what the room felt like, whether the dog was in the way. This is ethnographic work in a small number of sites and it does not claim generalisability, but it establishes that the unit of analysis in change work is the encounter rather than the intention behind it.

Taken together, this literature establishes that the worker's behaviour matters and that the profession does not currently describe, supervise or record it in terms specific enough to be improved. What it leaves open is what those terms should be. That is the question the behavioural science literature has spent forty years answering for other professions.

## Motivation as a property of the conversation

The most portable framework in the health literature is COM-B. Michie, van Stralen and West (2011) argue that any behaviour requires capability, opportunity and motivation, and that an intervention that leaves one of the three unaddressed will not produce the behaviour. The framework was built to design health interventions and to characterise existing ones, and its authors present it as an organising tool rather than a validated causal model.

Its usefulness in child protection is diagnostic. A plan that requires a parent to attend a domestic abuse programme addresses capability, in the sense of teaching something, and often nothing else. Whether the parent can get there, whether the sessions clash with a contact arrangement, whether attending requires disclosing to an employer, and whether the parent has any reason of their own to go, are opportunity and motivation questions that the plan usually does not ask. Michie and colleagues (2013) went on to produce a taxonomy of 93 distinct behaviour change techniques, built for consistent reporting of interventions. Social work plans routinely specify an outcome and leave the technique unspecified, which is why two workers can deliver the same plan and do entirely different things.

On motivation itself, the two most useful bodies of work agree with each other and with what good practitioners already do. Ryan and Deci (2000) argue that motivation is a state produced by conditions rather than a quantity a person possesses, and that autonomy, competence and relatedness are the conditions that produce the self-sustaining kind. Bandura (1977) had already made the narrower point that beliefs about one's own capacity to act determine whether a person begins, how hard they try and how long they persist under difficulty. Both are heavily cited theoretical positions from psychology rather than findings from child protection settings, and neither was developed under conditions of statutory coercion.

Motivational interviewing takes the same position into method. Miller and Rollnick (2023) set out four tasks, engaging, focusing, evoking and planning, and the central technical move is evoking the person's own reasons for change rather than supplying the worker's. In the English child and family context, Forrester and colleagues (2019) provide the strongest available evidence that these skills relate to how families experience the work.

The honest reading of that evidence is mixed. A randomised trial of a training and supervision package in motivational interviewing raised workers' skills by about half a point on a five-point scale, and that improvement did not translate into different outcomes for families `[VERIFY: full citation for the RCT reported in this literature; do not cite from this summary alone]`. Two readings are available and the literature does not settle between them. Either the skill gain was too small to matter, or the outcome measures were too blunt to detect what changed. Both readings should be held.

There is a further problem that the health literature cannot help with. Motivational interviewing rests on supporting autonomy, and a social worker in a child protection process is limiting autonomy by statute. The worker asks a parent what they want for their children while holding the power to initiate proceedings if the answer is wrong. Miller and Rollnick's method was developed in addictions work where the person could leave. Nothing in the technique dissolves that contradiction, and practitioners who use motivational interviewing in statutory work are using an autonomy-supporting method inside an autonomy-limiting mandate. The contradiction is real and should be named in supervision rather than resolved by pretending the mandate is not there.

For practice, the immediate consequence is that a worker who records "parent lacks motivation" has recorded nothing usable. The question is which condition is absent: a reason of the parent's own, a belief that they could succeed, or a relationship in which the goal can be discussed without the parent losing face.

## Repetition, context and the timescale of habit

The habit literature is where behavioural science is most specific and where social work planning is most vague.

Wood and Neal (2007) model habits as associations between a context and a response, learned gradually through repetition, such that perception of the context triggers the response without a mediating goal. The practical consequence is that a habit, once formed, no longer depends on the motivation that built it. Gardner, Lally and Wardle (2012) put the same point to general practitioners: automaticity is worth aiming for because it persists when attention, motivation and circumstances deteriorate.

That is a claim social work should take seriously, because deterioration is the norm in the families it works with. A routine that holds only while the parent is trying hard is a routine that will fail in the week the benefit payment is late.

On timescale, Lally and colleagues (2010) asked 96 volunteers to perform a chosen eating, drinking or activity behaviour daily in the same context for twelve weeks, measuring self-reported automaticity each day. Time to reach 95 per cent of maximum automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a median of 66. Missing a single opportunity did not measurably damage the process.

Three things follow, and the third is the one that matters.

First, the range is wide and the median is not a target. Second, the finding that one missed day does not undo the work is directly relevant to how plans and reports are written, since practice tends to read a single missed appointment as evidence about a parent's commitment rather than as noise in a learning curve. Third, and this is where the study has to be tested rather than applied, these were 96 self-selected volunteers choosing their own simple behaviour in a stable daily context, most of them adopting something like eating fruit with lunch. A parent on a child protection plan has not chosen the behaviour, is often being asked to change several at once, and frequently has no stable context to attach the behaviour to. Temporary accommodation, shift work, contact arrangements and unpredictable income all remove the thing habit formation runs on.

So the honest transfer is narrow. What the habit literature establishes for social work is that repetition in a stable context is the mechanism, that stability of context is a precondition rather than a detail, and that a parent whose life has no stable context cannot form habits regardless of how motivated they are. The 66 days is a median from one study of volunteers and it should not be read as a timescale for anything. That reframes housing and income as clinical variables rather than background circumstances.

Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) supply the most usable technique. Their meta-analysis of 94 independent tests with more than 8,000 participants found a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) of implementation intentions on goal attainment. An implementation intention specifies when, where and how a person will act: if situation Y occurs, I will do X. The effect held for initiating action, protecting it from distraction and disengaging from failing strategies. The studies are overwhelmingly laboratory and health-behaviour based, and the meta-analysis predates the reproducibility debates that have revised effect size estimates across social psychology, so the figure should be treated as an upper bound rather than a promise.

Even discounted, the technique is close to free and it is precisely what most child protection plans lack. "Mum will ensure the children attend school regularly" specifies an outcome. "On school mornings, when the alarm goes at seven, Mum will take Kayden downstairs before she checks her phone" specifies a cue, an action and a context, and it can be reviewed against something other than opinion.

There is a cost to writing plans this way, and it should be stated. Specificity increases the surveillance of small domestic acts, and it hands the worker a more detailed instrument for finding a parent wanting. A plan that names the cue also names the moment of failure. Practitioners writing at this level of detail are increasing their power, not reducing it, and the ethical answer is that the parent authors the implementation intention and the worker records it in the parent's words.

## Shame: the condition that determines whether help can be received

Everything above assumes a person who can discuss their own behaviour with a professional. The shame literature explains why that assumption often fails, and this is the point at which the behavioural sciences need social work more than social work needs them.

Tangney and Dearing (2002) draw the distinction the rest of the field works from. Guilt attaches to an act and tends to produce repair. Shame attaches to the whole self and tends to produce concealment, withdrawal and, when cornered, anger. This is a distinction with a substantial empirical base in psychology, though built largely on non-clinical American samples using self-report measures.

Brown (2006), working in social work, developed shame resilience theory from a grounded theory study of women and shame, and identified speaking shame within a connected relationship as the route out of it. The study is single-gender, American and qualitative, and it should not be read as an effectiveness claim. What it offers is a mechanism: shame is reduced by being known and not rejected, which is an account of what a helping relationship does that goes beyond warmth.

Gibson (2015) brings this into English child protection directly. He argues that shame is intrinsic to the material of the work and that the child protection process itself generates it for parents and for workers. His later case study of an English child protection service (Gibson, 2020) found that practice frequently evoked or intensified shame and humiliation in parents, and that this was produced by the ordinary operation of the process rather than by poor individual practice. It is a single-service qualitative study and its transferability to other authorities is untested, which the author acknowledges.

Munford and Sanders (2020), working with young people in New Zealand who had experienced chronic adversity and multiple services, reach a compatible conclusion from the other end of the age range, and pair shame with recognition as its counterpart. The jurisdictional difference matters, since the New Zealand system carries a different relationship to indigenous families and a different statutory architecture, but the mechanism they describe is the same one.

Shame is also structural. Walker (2014) presents cross-national evidence that shame is a component of poverty rather than a reaction to it, and Chase and Walker (2013), drawing on qualitative work with adults in poverty in the United Kingdom, describe shame as co-constructed, produced by being shamed as much as by feeling ashamed. Featherstone, White and Morris (2014), and later Featherstone, Gupta, Morris and White (2018), argue that English child protection has developed a culture that individualises the problems of deprived families and treats parents as the object of rescue operations. These are polemical works of argument rather than empirical studies, and they have been contested within the profession, but the empirical case for the underlying inequality is separately established: Bywaters and colleagues (2020), in the Child Welfare Inequalities Project, demonstrate a steep gradient in intervention rates by neighbourhood deprivation across the four UK nations.

Put the strands together and the analytic point for practice is this. A parent in a child protection meeting is being asked to receive help, in public, from an organisation that has already communicated that their parenting is not good enough, while being poor in a society that treats poverty as a personal failure, in a process whose outcome may be the removal of their child. The behavioural sciences would call the resulting concealment non-engagement or resistance and look for a technique to overcome it. The shame literature reads it as a person protecting a self that is under threat, which is a different problem with a different response.

This is also where the cost to the worker becomes visible. Workers manage their own shame in this process, in supervision, in audit and in serious case review. A worker who has been shamed for a missed visit becomes a worker who documents defensively, and defensive documentation is written for the record rather than for the family. The organisational culture that shames practitioners is producing the practice that shames parents, and both are produced by the same anxiety about blame.

For practice, this means the first behavioural task in most statutory cases is making help receivable. Nothing in the habit or motivation literature works on a person who is concealing.

## Bandwidth, friction and the limits of the individual frame

The last body of work explains a category of practice failure that is regularly misread as attitude.

Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir and Zhao (2013) report two studies in *Science*. In the first, inducing thoughts about finances reduced cognitive performance in poor participants but not in well-off ones. In the second, the same Indian farmers performed worse on cognitive tests before harvest, when poor, than after harvest, when relatively rich. The effect is within-person, which rules out the explanation that less cognitively able people become poor. The work has been contested in the same journal and effect sizes in this literature have been revised, so it should be cited as evidence that scarcity consumes cognitive capacity rather than as a precise quantity.

The practice implication is direct. Appointment-keeping, form-filling, phone-answering and remembering multi-agency arrangements are exactly the functions scarcity degrades. A service that responds to missed appointments by adding more appointments is adding load to the system that is already failing.

The corresponding technique in behavioural science is friction. Making an intended behaviour easier, and making the unintended one harder, produces effects that exhortation does not. In practice this is booking the appointment at the school gate rather than the office, sending the reminder the parent will actually see, filling in the form together, and removing the requirement to tell the story again to a fourth professional.

Chater and Loewenstein (2023) set the boundary. Their argument is that behavioural public policy has focused on individual-level fixes, the i-frame, where systemic change, the s-frame, was what the problem required, and that the individual framing has at times been actively promoted to forestall structural reform. The paper is a target article in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, published with substantial peer commentary that disputes parts of it, and it concerns policy rather than casework. Its relevance here is as a warning about scope. A social worker who teaches a parent implementation intentions for morning routines while the family is in a single room with no cooking facilities has applied the mechanism at the wrong level.

English policy has arrived at a version of the same conclusion. MacAlister (2022) argued for a reset toward family help and support, and the government's subsequent strategy and the revised statutory guidance (Department for Education, 2023) reorganise multi-agency arrangements around help as well as protection. Whether that reorganisation reaches families is an empirical question that is not yet answered, and Bywaters and colleagues (2020) found spending on keeping families together falling as a proportion of children's services budgets while spending on care and investigation rose, with the most deprived authorities hit hardest.

## What the mechanism looks like when it is named

The literature reviewed here supports a specific account of how change happens in statutory family work. Four conditions have to hold, and they hold in order.

**Help has to be receivable.** Shame determines whether a parent can talk about their own behaviour at all. The worker's task is to reduce the shame the process generates, which means naming the power they hold rather than performing its absence, separating the act from the person in the way plans and reports are written, and giving the parent something to be recognised for. Nothing else in this list functions before this one does.

**The behaviour has to be the parent's, and specified.** Motivation is a state produced by conditions, of which autonomy is one, so the target behaviour and the reason for it come from the parent through evoking rather than from the worker through instruction. The behaviour is then specified to the level of a cue, an action and a context, in the parent's words.

**The context has to be stable enough to repeat in.** Habits form through repetition in a consistent context. Where the context is unstable, because of housing, income, shift patterns or contact arrangements, stabilising the context is the intervention and the behavioural work waits. This is the point at which practice has to act at the level of the family's material conditions rather than their intentions.

**The friction has to come off.** Scarcity consumes the cognitive capacity that appointments and paperwork demand. Removing steps from the intended behaviour does more than increasing the number of requirements.

The fifth element is not about the family. **The worker's behaviour is the intervention**, which means it is the thing that has to be observed, described and improved. At present it is largely absent from the record. Supervision, on the available evidence, examines the case rather than the practice, and plans record what a parent must achieve rather than what the worker will do.

That last asymmetry is worth stating plainly, because it is the one this review has most confidence in. A child protection plan is a document about a parent's behaviour, written by a professional whose own behaviour is the least documented variable in the case.

## What follows for practice

**For practitioners**

- Write plan actions as cue, action and context, in the parent's words, and record who authored each one.
- Ask what would make the action easier before asking what would make the parent more committed.
- Treat a single missed action as noise rather than as evidence about the parent, and say so in the recording.
- Where a family's living context cannot support repetition, name that in the plan as the reason the behavioural target is being deferred, rather than recording non-compliance.
- Name the power you hold in the first conversation. Shame is worse where the power is unspoken.

**For organisations and supervisors**

- Make the worker's behaviour a standing item in supervision, using an observational method rather than case narrative.
- Audit plans for whether the technique is specified, not only whether the outcome is.
- Read patterns of non-engagement as service design data before reading them as family characteristics.
- Examine what the organisation does with practitioner error, since the culture that shames workers produces the documentation that shames parents.

**For research**

- Can implementation intentions, authored by parents, be used in child protection plans without increasing surveillance of parents? What do parents say about being asked to write them?
- What is the timescale of habit formation for parents living with unstable housing and income, and how does it compare with the timescales set by plans and by court?
- Does observed worker behaviour predict family outcomes when outcomes are measured beyond parent self-report at a single point?
- What would a shame-reducing child protection process look like at the level of process design, and can it be tested against the current one?

## What this review does not claim

It does not claim that behavioural techniques produce change in child protection. That has not been demonstrated, and the strongest adjacent evidence, on motivational interviewing skills, found a skill gain that did not move family outcomes.

It does not claim that the four conditions above operate in the order given for every family, only that the literature supports that ordering as a default and that shame is the constraint most often left unaddressed.

It does not claim generalisability beyond England, and the habit and scarcity evidence comes from populations who differ from the families in question in almost every respect that matters.

It offers no resolution to the contradiction at the centre of the argument. Autonomy-supporting methods are being recommended for use inside a mandate that limits autonomy, by workers who can remove children. That contradiction is a permanent feature of statutory practice, and a paper that resolved it would be describing a different job.
